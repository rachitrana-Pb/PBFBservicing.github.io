The part of product management nobody sees

The machine now writes the spec, the summary and the ticket before the kettle boils. Which finally forces an honest question about what the rest of the role was ever for.

In short

  • What remains is harder, deciding between people who are each correct from where they stand.

Every few weeks someone asks whether AI is going to take my job. Always kindly, and almost always by an engineer who has just watched a model produce in forty seconds a requirement document that would have cost me an afternoon.

The honest answer is that it already has. Just not the part anyone warned me about.

What actually went away

For most of my four years in product, a large share of the week went into paperwork. Requirement documents. Release notes. Ticket descriptions with acceptance criteria spelled out three ways so nobody could plead ambiguity later. Mails restating what everyone had just agreed to out loud. Decks that existed only because someone senior wanted the same facts in a different shape.

None of it was thinking. It was transcription wearing a lanyard — exactly the sort of work a model does well, cheaply, at two in the morning, without getting resentful.

It came to roughly sixty hours a month of my own time. I do not miss a minute of it. What caught me off guard was the aftermath, the paperwork vanished and the job got heavier, because what remained was the part the paperwork had been politely hiding.

Five teams, five truths, one release

In corporate insurance servicing, one request rarely arrives from one place. Operations wants self-serve upload, because their team is drowning in mails. A stakeholder wants an approval gate and a single consolidated dashboard over the same flow, because they are the ones who answer the phone when a claim is rejected. The endorsement team wants one-click handling of a field the insurer's file format assumes and our system treats as optional. The issuance team wants the manual process handed to an agentic workflow that closes cases end to end. Sales wants all of it this quarter, because a large account is watching.

Every one of those positions is correct from the chair that team is sitting in. No dataset settles it, because the disagreement was never about information. It is an argument about how much risk the company will swallow, and who eats it when the bet is wrong.

The part that does not compress

A model can hold every constraint in the room at once. It cannot decide which of five correct teams gets told no, or carry the consequence of getting that wrong.

Deciding is not the difficult bit. Anyone can pick. The difficulty is picking in a way the person who lost can live with, then staying close enough that they still come to you first when the next thing catches fire. No sprint report has a column for that.

The line and the loop

On paper, product work is a straight line. Talk to users, write the requirement, prioritise, engineering builds it, ship and measure. Five boxes, one direction, no weather.

In practice it doubles back for months. Three stakeholders want three things. Sales adds a fourth. The estimate returns at three times what you promised, because nobody opened the schema. You cut scope, christen it phase one, then burn a fortnight re-arguing the cut after it escalates. You ship half. The data comes back shrugging — the users you built for logged in once. Adoption looked fine, retention did not. And the problem that finally mattered had been sitting fourth on the list the entire time.

For years I read that loop as evidence I was bad at planning. I have stopped apologising for it. The loop is the job — what learning looks like when the subject is people who cannot describe what they need until shown something wrong.

On the last few releases I have counted, it took four or five laps before the problem that actually mattered came into view. AI makes each lap dramatically faster. It has not removed a single one.

What I want the time for

The point of the reclaimed hours is not twice as many artefacts. It is the inputs that were always undersupplied.

  • Sitting with operations through a real batch, instead of reading their summary of it.
  • Watching an HR admin use the portal without helping her. Uncomfortable, and worth every minute.
  • Asking the engineer pushing back what he sees that I do not. He is usually right, and early.
  • Reopening a six-month-old decision to find out whether it worked, when nobody is asking.
  • Saying no to a request early enough that the no is still cheap.

All of it is slow, unphotogenic, and impossible to show off in a status update. All of it is where the judgement is quietly manufactured that makes the fast calls, months later, look obvious.

The question underneath

So, will AI replace product managers? It will take a real share of what we spend the week doing, and that is good news, because most of it was never the work. It was the residue the work left behind.

What it cannot do is sit in a room where five teams are each correct, absorb the cost of choosing between them, and still be standing there a quarter later when the choice is graded. It cannot decide that something is not worth building at all — the most valuable thing a product person produces, and the only one that leaves nothing behind.

So the next time an engineer asks what I actually do, the answer holds up better than it used to. I talk to people, I decide, and I stay in the room long enough to find out whether I was right.
